\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Between July and October~2026, every major Lightning implementation disclosed security vulnerabilities. Lightning
Labs published ten LND advisories on a single day~\cite{lightninglabs2026aug} and eight more a month
later~\cite{lightninglabs2026sep}; Core Lightning (CLN) asked operators who could not upgrade to restart their nodes
offline~\cite{coreln2026offline}; Eclair and LDK shipped security releases. Each advisory ends with the same advice:
upgrade. Whether operators do, and how fast, decides how long a disclosed vulnerability stays exploitable. For web
servers, browsers and Bitcoin and Ethereum full nodes, this question has been measured for two decades
\cite{arbaugh2000windows,rescorla2003securityholes,frei2009firefox,durumeric2014heartbleed,kim2018ethereum}. For the
Lightning Network it has not, because Lightning gossip carries no software version: earlier studies could tell which
implementation a node runs~\cite{zabka2021nodeclassification,espinasa2024decisiontree,kumar2026clientdiversity}, not
which release.

We show that the release can be read from gossip after all, and use it to ask who patches the Lightning Network:
operators on their own schedule, the platforms that package the implementations, or the advisories themselves. Each
release announces a characteristic set of protocol feature bits. From the source of every release of LND, CLN, Eclair
and LDK since 2019 we derive which sets each release can produce, and match them against \DataUsableDays{} daily
snapshots of the public graph taken by our own node from May~2023 to October~2026. The answer is that the network
patches on release and platform schedules, not on disclosures. Most operators upgrade within months of a release;
the largest movements are platforms shipping a release to all their users at once; and an advisory, published long
after its fix, moves at most the operators who were already close to current. We make four contributions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Release-line inference from gossip.} We infer the release line (the finest unit gossip separates;
patch releases are invisible) of every public node with a known implementation. On \NVerNodes{} CLN nodes whose alias
states their version, the inferred range contains the true line on \PctVerStrict\% of \NVerNodeDays{} node-days and
the most likely line is exact for \NVerNodeLinesExact{} of \NVerNodeLines{} node--line pairs. For LND, which has no
such ground truth, three checks that need none agree: line decreases are \PctDownLND\% of observed line changes, no
LND line released since 2024 was seen on more than \PreRelLndMax{} nodes before its publication, and
\PlatLineMin--\PlatLineMax\% of the nodes that changed line right after a platform shipped a new LND line landed on
the line it shipped (\S\ref{sec:inference}).
\item \textbf{Upgrade dynamics.} With a managed fleet of up to \FleetPeakNodes{} live LND
nodes~\cite{kumar2026clientdiversity} set apart, new lines reach half of live LND nodes in
\TFiftyLndMin--\TFiftyLndMax{} days. Half of the LND nodes on an older line upgrade within \MedianLNDAll{} days of a
release, but the smallest quarter of nodes takes \MedianLNDSmall{}, and a node already more than one line behind has
about five times lower odds of moving in any week (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).
\item \textbf{Platforms set the pace.} We date the LND and CLN releases of two node platforms to the minute from
public records. The three largest LND upgrade waves, \WaveUmbrelMinNodes--\WaveUmbrelMaxNodes{} nodes each, re-signed
their announcements a median \WaveUmbrelHoursMin--\WaveUmbrelHoursMax{}~hours after the Umbrel app store published a
new LND version, and another wave followed BTCPay Server's security release. Upgrades signed within 48~hours after
such a release are \PlatShareLND\% of all LND upgrades since June~2025, although those windows cover \PlatTimeLND\% of
the time (\S\ref{sec:waves}).
\item \textbf{Disclosure, response and exposure.} For the \NAdvTotal{} LND and CLN advisories whose fix boundary
gossip can see, we measure how many live nodes and how much capacity remained exposed. Fixes typically shipped months
before disclosure (median \FixToDiscMedian{} days). For \NResp{} disclosures we compare the weekly rate at which exposed
nodes moved to a later line before and after disclosure, with and without the moves that platform releases explain.
Only CLN's July~2026 disclosures raised it, by a factor of \RROpClnRecent{} among nodes one line behind; nodes
further behind moved when BTCPay's own advisory had their operators update. No LND disclosure produced a detectable
increase, and \PctCohortStillLLBatch\% of the nodes exposed to Lightning Labs' August~2026 batch were still live on an affected
release two months later. When CLN asked operators who could not upgrade to restart offline, nodes already on the
newest line went offline at twice the rate of nodes behind (\OffCallNewestLeft\% against \OffCallBehindLeft\%)
(\S\ref{sec:exposure}).
\end{itemize}

All data behind the figures and tables, and the code, are released. We report aggregates only: no list, ranking or
label of individual nodes by release, platform or exposure.
