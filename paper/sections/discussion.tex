\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Platforms, not advisories, move nodes}
The largest upgrade events in our data are platform pushes. Each time the Umbrel app store shipped a new
LND line, \WaveUmbrelMinNodes--\WaveUmbrelMaxNodes{} nodes re-announced within about a day; BTCPay Server's security
release moved \WaveBTCPayNodes{} LND nodes in a day, and with them the CLN nodes on old lines that CLN's own
disclosure had not moved. No LND disclosure produced a visible increase at all. Frei et al.\ found the same for browsers:
the update mechanism, more than the user, sets the patch level~\cite{frei2009firefox}; and Sarabi et al.\ found the
vendor's patch speed to matter more than users' promptness~\cite{sarabi2017patchme}. On the Lightning Network the
vendor that reaches most small nodes is the platform, not the implementation. Each Umbrel wave came
\WaveUmbrelDaysMin--\WaveUmbrelDaysMax{} days after LND's own release, a large part of the median time to upgrade
(\MedianLNDAll{} days). Two practical consequences follow. A fix reaches most small nodes when platforms ship it, so
the platforms' release schedule, not only the implementation's, decides how long a vulnerability stays open. And a
platform that tells its users to update moves even nodes that ignored earlier releases: \WaveBTCPayOlder{} of the
\WaveBTCPayNodes{} nodes in the BTCPay wave had skipped at least one LND line, although the advisory that moved them
concerned BTCPay rather than LND.

\paragraph{Disclosure reaches the operators who were already upgrading}
The LND advisories of 2025 and 2026 were published long after the fixes shipped (median \FixToDiscMedian{} days across
the LND and CLN advisories), a common practice that lets operators upgrade before attackers learn what to look for. It works for
the operators who follow releases: half of LND nodes move to a new line within three months, and the largest move
first. The nodes still on an affected line at disclosure are the ones that did not, and for LND the disclosure did not
detectably change their pace: \PctCohortStillLLBatch\% of those exposed to Lightning Labs' August batch were still exposed two
months later. The one disclosure that did move operators makes the same point from the other side. CLN's July
disclosures raised the rate of leaving affected lines \RROpClnRecent-fold among nodes one line behind, operators who
keep up; the nodes further behind moved when BTCPay's advisory had their operators update. CLN's emergency call of
26~August selected the same way: the nodes that went offline were mostly on the newest line, already patched against
every vulnerability CLN had disclosed, while most nodes still exposed to the July vulnerabilities stayed online. Read
through release lines, the shutdown we measured earlier~\cite{kumar2026clientdiversity} was followed mainly by the
operators who were already keeping up.
Reported attacks do not
explain the CLN response, since the maintainers reported attacks on unpatched nodes only in
October~\cite{coreln2026attacks}. More plausibly, a fix that had shipped \FixToDiscClnGossipd{} days before disclosure
found most exposed CLN nodes one line behind, while one shipped \FixToDiscLLBatch{} days before found only the LND
nodes that had skipped every release since. Notification studies on the Internet find that most notified operators
never act~\cite{li2016notifications} and that direct notification raised Heartbleed patching by about
half~\cite{durumeric2014heartbleed}. A Lightning advisory is not a notification: it is a post that reaches those who
read the implementation's channels, and the operators who do not are reached, if at all, by their platform.
Release-line inference also gives maintainers a measurement they have lacked: the share of live nodes and capacity
still on an affected line, read in aggregate from public gossip, which can inform when to disclose.

\paragraph{The tail}
What remains exposed are small nodes, nodes that skipped earlier releases, and nodes that no platform updates. For
advisories that require a channel with the victim, these nodes are also the cheapest to reach. Their share of
capacity at risk is small (\ExpCapLLBatchDisc\% of live LND capacity for the August~2026 LND batch), but each holds its
operator's funds.

\paragraph{Counting nodes}
At its peak, one coordinated fleet was more than a quarter of the live LND nodes we observe, with almost no
capacity. Any statistic that counts nodes, whether implementation shares, version shares or exposure, would have been
dominated by that fleet's schedule. Fleets are cheap to create and hard to see. This one gave itself away by a shared
alias and hubs~\cite{kumar2026clientdiversity} and, here, by upgrading in step; a fleet with neither would pass for
hundreds of independent operators. Node-level Lightning measurements should look for such fleets before counting,
and report capacity alongside nodes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\emph{Release lines, not patches.} Gossip cannot separate patch releases, so fixes inside a line, including all
of CLN's 2026 patch releases, cannot be measured; for advisories with a fix inside a line we report bounds.
\emph{Inference.} An operator can switch features on or off so that an older line looks like a newer one or the
reverse; the strict range covers every non-developer configuration, and validation on self-identifying CLN nodes
bounds the error of the likely line. For LND we have no ground truth at scale, only the three checks of
\S\ref{sec:validation}. A node that upgrades without re-announcing, or whose announcement we do not receive, keeps its
old bits, which biases exposure up.
\emph{Platforms.} We can date the releases of two platforms; upgrades pushed by others count as operators' own, so
platform shares are lower bounds. Dropping every move in the 48~hours after a platform release also drops operators
who moved on their own in those hours, which makes the ratios outside platform releases conservative.
\emph{Response design.} The before/after comparison is biased towards ratios below one when a line is young. With
\NResp{} disclosures, a few weeks on each side and moves that cluster, the intervals are wide: a ratio near one rules
out a large response, not a small one.
\emph{Vantage and liveness.} All data come from one node's view of the public graph; unannounced nodes, including
most mobile wallets, are not covered, and liveness is inferred from peers' channel updates.
\emph{Fleets.} We identified one managed fleet by its alias and hubs. Smaller fleets without a shared alias would go
undetected and be counted as independent nodes.
\emph{Exposure is not exploitability.} Several advisories require a channel with the victim, a particular
configuration or inbound connections; we count nodes on affected releases, not nodes that can be attacked. Our
advisory list is compiled from public sources and may miss silent fixes.
\emph{Association.} The hazard model describes which nodes upgrade sooner; it does not identify why.

\section{Ethics}
\label{sec:ethics}
Release-line inference identifies nodes running vulnerable software, and in 2026 Lightning deployments were
exploited (BTCPay Server~\cite{btcpay2026advisory}) and CLN maintainers reported attacks on unpatched
nodes~\cite{coreln2026attacks}. We therefore publish aggregates only: no list, ranking, filter or label of individual
nodes by release, platform or exposure, in the paper or in the released data. All inputs are public gossip and public
release records, every advisory we study had been published before our analysis began, and the paper reports no new
vulnerability.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Lightning gossip carries no version string, but the feature bits each release announces identify its release line
closely enough to measure who patches the network. Operators patch on release schedules: most upgrade within a few
months of a release. Platforms patch on theirs: the largest single movements are node platforms shipping a release
to all their users at once. Disclosures, which typically come long after the fix, move few nodes: of those we
can test, only Core Lightning's of July~2026 raised the pace, and only among operators who were already close to
current. A tail of small and neglected nodes stays on vulnerable releases. Measured by capacity it is small; measured
by nodes, it depends on whether one counts a single fleet as hundreds of operators. For the maintainers of Lightning
implementations, the lever that moves the tail is the platforms that package their releases.
